# Business Communication Frameworks: A Working Reference

Scope: 15 frameworks most relevant to **executive updates/status reports** and **strategic recommendations**, plus overlay lenses (Ethos/Pathos/Logos, WIIFM) that don't sequence a message but change how you weight or frame it. Each is covered three ways: a one-look summary, a side-by-side comparison, and a full explainer with sourcing. Part 4 covers the Ethos/Pathos/Logos overlay; Part 5 maps every framework to the communication intent it best serves (Clarify / Decide / Act / Engage).

DECIDE, PSAB, ABT and 5 Ws/5W1H, plus the WIIFM overlay and the intent map in Part 5, were added based on a framework catalog a colleague shared from an internal agent design ("LOGOS Strategic Comm Agent"). Where that catalog's description of a framework matched independently verifiable sources, it's cited normally; where it didn't (noted per-framework below), that's called out explicitly rather than presented as a well-established public model.

The last four entries — Five Factors, Book of Five Rings, Six Thinking Hats and Tao Te Ching — come from classical strategy and thinking texts rather than the business-communication literature. Six Thinking Hats is a published method for exactly this kind of use. The other three are genuine and much-translated works whose framing *as communication frameworks* is this catalog's adaptation, and they are labelled as such in their explainers rather than presented as established models.

## Part 1 — Executive Summaries

**SCIPAB** (Situation, Complication, Implication, Position, Action, Benefit) — A six-step persuasion structure used to build a business case: lay out the facts, name what's changed, spell out the cost of doing nothing, state your recommendation, define the ask, and close with the payoff. Best for strategic recommendations where you need to build the case, not just state the answer.

**SWOT** (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) — A 2x2 assessment splitting internal factors (strengths/weaknesses) from external ones (opportunities/threats). It's an analysis tool, not a narrative structure — it tells you *what* to say, not *how* to sequence it. Pair it with SCQA or SCIPAB to present the findings.

**Minto Pyramid Principle / SCQA** — Lead with the answer, then support it with grouped, logically-ordered arguments (McKinsey's house style). The opening is framed as Situation → Complication → Question → Answer. Best default for exec memos and recommendation docs where the reader wants the conclusion in the first ten seconds.

**STAR** (Situation, Task, Action, Result) — A retrospective structure for reporting what happened: context, what was required, what you did, what resulted. Best for project status updates and performance narratives ("here's what we did and what it produced"). Not built for forward-looking proposals.

**OGSM** (Objective, Goals, Strategies, Measures) — A strategic-planning framework: one qualitative ambition (Objective), quantified targets (Goals), the approaches to get there (Strategies), and the KPIs that track progress (Measures). Best for annual plans, team strategy documents, and board-level strategy narratives — overkill for a single update or ask.

**DECIDE** (Decision Required, Context, Options, Impact, Recommendation, Deadline) — Structures a message around getting one specific decision made: name the decision, give context, lay out the options, show the impact of each, recommend one, and attach a deadline. Best for approvals, budget asks, and escalations where the ask must be unambiguous. Note: this six-step, communication-oriented structure comes from the colleague-shared catalog — a different, unrelated "DECIDE" model (Detect, Estimate, Choose, Identify, Do, Evaluate) is the one more commonly documented publicly, originating in aviation decision training.

**PSAB** (Problem, Solution, Action, Benefit) — A shorter, action-first cousin of SCIPAB: state the problem, propose the solution, define the action needed, close with the benefit. Best when you need adoption or task completion and don't need SCIPAB's fuller Situation/Complication build-up. Note: the widely documented public version of this idea is the three-part "Problem-Solution-Benefit" (PSB); the explicit fourth "Action" step is specific to the colleague-shared catalog.

**WIIFM** (What's In It For Me) — Not a sequence but an audience-relevance lens: reframe any message around what this specific audience gains, risks, or cares about. Best applied on top of another framework's benefit/action step whenever stakeholder buy-in is uncertain — treat it the same way as the Ethos/Pathos/Logos layer in Part 4.

**ABT** (And, But, Therefore) — A three-beat narrative template: establish agreed context (And), introduce the tension or problem (But), deliver the resolution (Therefore). Best for messages that need to feel like a story — kickoffs, change announcements, all-hands — rather than a status report.

**5 Ws / 5W1H** (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How) — A fact-gathering checklist for building a complete picture of a situation before you communicate about it. Best used as prep before drafting — kickoffs, incident summaries, confirming you have the full picture before recommending anything.

**Rhetorical Appeals** (Ethos, Logos, Pathos, Kairos) — Aristotle's persuasive appeals used as a checklist rather than a sequence: is the case credible, sound, felt, and timely? Best for pressure-testing an argument before it goes out — catching a case leaning entirely on data when the room also needs to trust you, or entirely on urgency when the room wants proof. Part 4 covers the same material as a weighting layer across the other frameworks; this entry is the board-level check.

**Five Factors** (Art of War — The Way, Heaven, Earth, Command, Method) — Sun Tzu's five criteria for assessing a contest before committing to it: shared will, conditions you don't control, the terrain you actually occupy, the quality of leadership, and whether the organization behind the plan can deliver. Best for market entries, competitive bids, and turf contests. Not a communication sequence so much as an assessment you then present.

**Book of Five Rings** (Ground, Water, Fire, Wind, Void) — Musashi's five-part strategic progression: foundation, adaptability, direct engagement, study of rivals, and the intuitive judgment that goes beyond technique. Best for longer strategy narratives that need to show both discipline and the non-obvious call. Overkill for a quick ask.

**Six Thinking Hats** (White, Red, Black, Yellow, Green, Blue) — De Bono's parallel-thinking method: the group deliberately adopts one mode at a time — facts, feelings, caution, optimism, alternatives, process — instead of arguing across modes at once. Best for structured group reviews that otherwise collapse into all-criticism or all-enthusiasm too early.

**Tao Te Ching** (The Way, Wu Wei, the case for restraint) — A lens for arguing *against* action: identify the situation's natural tendency, find the smallest intervention that works with it rather than against it, and make the case that less wins here. Best when the instinct in the room is to add process, headcount, or control. Pair it with a framework that states what should actually be done.

## Part 2 — Comparison Matrix

| Framework | Primary Use | Structure | Prep Time | Best When | Avoid When |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SCIPAB | Strategic recommendation / business case | 6-step narrative build | Medium-High | You need to justify *why* before stating *what* | Audience already agrees on direction — too long |
| SWOT | Situational/strategic analysis | 2x2 grid (not sequential) | Medium | Assessing internal capability vs. external landscape | Used alone as a narrative — no "so what" |
| Pyramid Principle / SCQA | Recommendation docs, exec memos, decks | Answer-first, MECE support | Medium-High | Reader wants the conclusion immediately | Delivering sensitive news that needs context first |
| STAR | Status reporting, performance narrative | Chronological (context → outcome) | Low-Medium | Reporting what was done and what it produced | Forward-looking, multi-option strategic asks |
| OGSM | Strategic planning, org/team alignment | Objective → Goals → Strategies → Measures | High | Setting or communicating multi-quarter/annual strategy | A one-off status update or single ask |
| DECIDE | Decision/approval request | 6-step, decision-first | Medium | You need a specific yes/no or a choice made | The message isn't actually asking for a decision |
| PSAB | Action-oriented ask | 4-step, shorter than SCIPAB | Low-Medium | You need adoption/action without the full business case | Audience needs the fuller "why now" case (use SCIPAB instead) |
| WIIFM | Audience relevance (overlay) | Not sequential | Low | Stakeholder buy-in is uncertain | Used alone — pair with a sequential framework |
| ABT | Narrative/storytelling | 3-beat: And → But → Therefore | Low | The message needs to feel like a story, not a report | Routine status updates or data-heavy asks |
| 5 Ws/5W1H | Fact-gathering / context-building | Checklist, not a narrative | Low | Before drafting anything, to confirm you have the full picture | As the final deliverable itself — it's prep, not the message |
| Rhetorical Appeals | Pressure-testing a persuasive case (overlay) | 4-part check, not sequential | Low | An argument is written and you want to find the gap | Used alone — it checks a case, it doesn't build one |
| Five Factors | Competitive assessment before committing | 5-factor assessment | Medium-High | Deciding whether to enter or continue a contest | Routine updates — it's a strategic judgment, not a report |
| Book of Five Rings | Long-form strategy narrative | 5-part progression | High | Strategy needing foundation *and* the non-obvious call | Short asks — the arc needs room to land |
| Six Thinking Hats | Structured group review of an idea | 6 modes, run in sequence | Medium | Discussion collapses into one mode too early | Solo drafting — it's built for a group |
| Tao Te Ching | Arguing for restraint or non-action | 3-part case for less | Low-Medium | The room's instinct is to add effort, process, or control | You need to state what *should* be done — pair with another |

**Quick decision guide:**
- Need to justify a recommendation to a senior audience → **SCIPAB** or **Pyramid Principle/SCQA**
- Need to update on progress of something already approved → **STAR**
- Need to assess a situation before recommending anything → **SWOT** (then wrap the output in SCQA or SCIPAB to present it)
- Need to judge whether to enter or continue a competitive fight → **Five Factors**
- Setting direction for a team, quarter, or year → **OGSM**, or **Book of Five Rings** when the strategy needs to show adaptability and judgment, not just targets
- Need a specific decision or approval made → **DECIDE**
- Need action/adoption without building the full case → **PSAB**
- Unsure whether stakeholders will care → apply **WIIFM** on top of whatever framework you're using
- Need the message to land as a story, not a report → **ABT**
- Need to confirm you have the full picture before writing anything → **5 Ws/5W1H**
- Need to run a group review without it collapsing into one mode → **Six Thinking Hats**
- Need to argue for doing less, or nothing → **Tao Te Ching**
- Written the case already and want to find its weak spot → **Rhetorical Appeals**

## Part 3 — Extended Explainers

### SCIPAB
**Full name:** Situation, Complication, Implication, Position, Action, Benefit
**Source:** Corporate communication training methodology; widely popularized in enterprise sales and consulting circles, including materials from Mandel Communications and Vantage Partners.
**Components:**
1. Situation — facts the audience already accepts as true.
2. Complication — what's changed, and why it's a problem.
3. Implication — the cost of not acting; creates urgency.
4. Position — your stated recommendation.
5. Action — the specific ask of the audience.
6. Benefit — the payoff if the action is taken.
**When to use:** Building a business case or strategic recommendation from scratch, especially when the audience needs to be walked through the "why" before the "what."
**When not to use:** Routine status updates, or audiences who already agree with the direction and just want the ask (use the Pyramid Principle/SCQA instead — lead with the answer).
**Example:** "Our support ticket backlog has grown 40% this quarter (Situation). Two senior engineers left and weren't backfilled (Complication). At this rate we breach our SLA with our top-3 accounts by Q3 (Implication). I recommend backfilling both roles and adding one contractor for 90 days (Position). I need budget approval by Friday (Action). This keeps us inside SLA and avoids escalation to the accounts' executive sponsors (Benefit)."
**Source:** [What is SCIPAB? A Communication Framework — Vantage Partners](https://www.vantagepartners.com/insights/what-is-scipab)

### SWOT
**Full name:** Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats
**Source:** Traced to research at the Stanford Research Institute (1960s–70s), commonly credited to Albert Humphrey, though the attribution is debated. Originally called "SOFT" analysis (Satisfactory, Opportunity, Fault, Threat) before evolving into SWOT.
**Components:**
- Strengths — internal, positive.
- Weaknesses — internal, negative.
- Opportunities — external, positive.
- Threats — external, negative.
**When to use:** Strategic planning sessions, competitive positioning discussions, evaluating a decision before recommending a course of action.
**When not to use:** As a stand-alone communication vehicle — SWOT organizes analysis, it doesn't sequence an argument. It needs to be wrapped in a narrative framework (SCQA, SCIPAB) before presenting to leadership.
**Example:** Used ahead of a market-entry recommendation to lay out internal capability (strengths/weaknesses) against external market conditions (opportunities/threats), then feeding the "so what" into a Pyramid Principle-structured recommendation.
**Source:** [The origins of SWOT analysis — ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0024630123000110)

### Minto Pyramid Principle / SCQA
**Full name:** Pyramid Principle, opened via Situation-Complication-Question-Answer (SCQA)
**Source:** Developed by Barbara Minto during her time at McKinsey (1960s–70s); codified in her book *The Pyramid Principle* (1996). It's the underlying structure behind most consulting-firm decks and memos.
**Components:**
- Lead with the answer/recommendation (top of the pyramid).
- Group supporting arguments so they are Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive (MECE).
- Order supporting ideas logically (deductive or inductive).
- Opening framed as: Situation (agreed context) → Complication (what changed) → Question (the question this raises) → Answer (your recommendation).
**When to use:** Recommendation memos, executive decks, any document where the reader has limited time and wants the conclusion first.
**When not to use:** Delivering sensitive or bad news that needs context and empathy before the headline; also weaker for exploratory or brainstorming conversations where the conclusion isn't set yet.
**Example:** A memo opens "We should exit the Northeast distributor partnership (Answer)" before laying out the three MECE reasons, rather than building up to that conclusion at the end.
**Source:** [ModelThinkers — Minto Pyramid & SCQA](https://modelthinkers.com/mental-model/minto-pyramid-scqa)

### STAR
**Full name:** Situation, Task, Action, Result
**Source:** Introduced by Development Dimensions International (DDI) in 1974 as part of its "Targeted Selection" behavioral interviewing methodology; since adapted well beyond interviews into feedback and status reporting.
**Components:**
1. Situation — the context.
2. Task — what needed to happen.
3. Action — what you/the team did.
4. Result — the outcome, ideally quantified.
**When to use:** Reporting on completed or in-flight initiatives, performance narratives, demonstrating impact of past work.
**When not to use:** Forward-looking strategic proposals with multiple options still on the table — STAR is retrospective by design.
**Example:** "Onboarding time was averaging 6 weeks, hurting time-to-productivity (Situation). We needed it under 3 weeks without adding headcount (Task). We redesigned the curriculum and automated provisioning (Action). Onboarding now averages 2.5 weeks, a 58% reduction (Result)."
**Source:** [STAR Method for Interviewing and Feedback — DDI](https://www.ddi.com/solutions/behavioral-interviewing/star-method)

### OGSM
**Full name:** Objective, Goals, Strategies, Measures
**Source:** Origins are debated — some trace it to strategic planning practices introduced in Japan during the post-WWII occupation era; Procter & Gamble is widely credited with modernizing and popularizing it in the 1990s under CEO A.G. Lafley. Since adopted across many Fortune 500 companies.
**Components:**
- Objective — one qualitative statement of ambition (the "why").
- Goals — quantitative targets tied to the objective.
- Strategies — the key approaches chosen to hit the goals.
- Measures — the KPIs used to track progress.
**When to use:** Annual or multi-quarter strategic plans, aligning a team or org around a single page of direction, board-level strategy narratives.
**When not to use:** A single status update or one-off ask — OGSM is a planning document, not a message format, and is overkill at that scale.
**Example:** Objective: "Become the top-rated vendor in customer support in our category." Goals: "Raise CSAT from 78 to 90 by Q4." Strategies: "Redesign escalation path; invest in tier-2 training." Measures: "CSAT score, escalation rate, first-response time."
**Source:** [OGSM — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OGSM)

### DECIDE
**Full name:** Decision Required, Context, Options, Impact, Recommendation, Deadline
**Source:** Shared by a colleague as part of an internal agent design's framework catalog ("LOGOS Strategic Comm Agent"). Flagged for transparency: a differently-structured "DECIDE" model (Detect, Estimate, Choose, Identify, Do, Evaluate) is the version more commonly documented in public sources, originating in aviation decision-making training and also referenced in some clinical decision contexts. The two share a name, not a structure. No independent public source for this six-step communication-oriented version was located — treat it as an internally-sourced framework unless your colleague can point to its origin.
**Components:**
1. Decision Required — name the specific decision being asked for.
2. Context — the background needed to evaluate it.
3. Options — the realistic choices on the table.
4. Impact — what each option means.
5. Recommendation — your stated choice.
6. Deadline or timing — when the decision is needed by.
**When to use:** Approval requests, budget asks, scope changes, and escalations where the audience needs to know a decision is being asked of them, not just informed.
**When not to use:** When you're not actually asking for a decision — a status update dressed up as a decision request will read as padded or confusing.
**Example:** "We need a call on the Q3 vendor contract by Friday (Decision Required). Current vendor's SLA has slipped twice this quarter (Context). We can renew as-is, renegotiate terms, or switch providers (Options). Renewing as-is repeats the risk; switching costs 6 weeks of migration (Impact). I recommend renegotiating (Recommendation), which needs their response by Thursday to hit our Friday deadline (Deadline)."
**Source:** internally sourced (colleague-shared catalog) — no independent public source verified; see caution above.

### PSAB
**Full name:** Problem, Solution, Action, Benefit
**Source:** Shared by a colleague as part of the same internal catalog. The publicly documented version of this idea is the three-part "Problem-Solution-Benefit" (PSB) framework, used in marketing and sales communication to structure a value proposition. PSAB's explicit fourth step (Action) doesn't appear in the public PSB sources found — treat the four-step version as the colleague's team's variant.
**Components:**
1. Problem — the issue being addressed.
2. Solution — the proposed fix.
3. Action — what the audience needs to do.
4. Benefit — why it's worth doing.
**When to use:** When you need people to adopt a plan, follow a new process, or complete a task, and the audience doesn't need the fuller "why now" build-up SCIPAB provides.
**When not to use:** When the audience is skeptical or the stakes are high enough that they need to see the cost of inaction (Implication) before they'll accept the ask — use SCIPAB instead.
**Example:** "Onboarding tickets are piling up in the shared queue (Problem). Moving to a triage rota fixes that (Solution). Sign up for one shift/week on the shared calendar by Friday (Action). This cuts response time in half without adding headcount (Benefit)."
**Source:** [PSB: Problem, solution, benefit — Deepstash](https://deepstash.com/idea/6799/psb-problem-solution-benefit) (three-part public version; four-part PSAB is internally sourced)

### WIIFM
**Full name:** What's In It For Me
**Source:** No single credited creator. The underlying idea — that persuasion works better when framed around the audience's self-interest — has informal roots in Dale Carnegie's 1936 *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, and the acronym itself became common usage in sales, marketing, and leadership-training circles through the mid-to-late 20th century.
**Components:** Not a sequence — a lens applied to any message. Before or while drafting, ask: what does this specific audience gain, risk, or care about, and is that made explicit?
**When to use:** Any time stakeholder buy-in is uncertain — change communications, asks that cost the audience time or effort, cross-functional requests where "why should I prioritize this" isn't obvious.
**When not to use:** As your only structure — WIIFM has no sequence of its own, so it needs to sit on top of a structural framework (most often the Benefit/Action step of SCIPAB or PSAB).
**Example:** Rewriting "We're rolling out a new expense tool" as "The new expense tool cuts your reimbursement wait from 3 weeks to 3 days" — same fact, reframed around what the audience actually gets.
**Source:** [WIIFM: Understanding the Power of "What's in It for Me" — InMotion Marketing](https://inmotionmktg.com/blog/wiifm-whats-in-it-for-me/)

### ABT
**Full name:** And, But, Therefore
**Source:** Developed by Randy Olson — a scientist-turned-filmmaker with a Harvard PhD who later earned an MFA in filmmaking — starting around 2011–2012, and formalized in his 2015 book *Houston, We Have a Narrative*. Olson traces the underlying pattern back to screenwriter Frank Daniel and, further, to Aristotelian narrative structure.
**Components:**
1. And — establish the agreed-upon context.
2. But — introduce the tension, conflict, or problem.
3. Therefore — deliver the resolution, conclusion, or action.
**When to use:** Messages that need narrative momentum rather than a report structure — kickoffs, all-hands announcements, change stories, anything where you want the message to be remembered, not just processed.
**When not to use:** Routine status updates or data-heavy asks, where the audience wants facts fast, not a narrative arc (use SCQA or STAR instead).
**Example:** "Our platform has served the same three markets for five years, and it's worked (And). But growth in all three has flattened for six straight quarters (But). Therefore, we're opening our first new market in eight years (Therefore)."
**Source:** [The ABT Framework](http://abtframework.com/)

### 5 Ws / 5W1H
**Full name:** Who, What, When, Where, Why, How
**Source:** Traces to ancient rhetorical traditions — the Greek rhetorician Hermagoras of Temnos described similar "elements of circumstance" in the 1st century BC, and English scholar Thomas Wilson outlined comparable questions in his 1560 treatise *The Arte of Rhetorique*. Popularized in its modern journalistic form from 1917 onward; the "How" is often credited to Rudyard Kipling's 1902 poem "The Elephant's Child."
**Components:** Who, What, When, Where, Why, How — asked systematically about a situation before writing about it.
**When to use:** Project kickoffs, incident summaries, crisis communication, or any time you need to confirm you have the complete picture before drafting a recommendation or update.
**When not to use:** As the communication itself — it's a discovery/prep tool, not a message structure; once you've answered all six, translate the findings into a structural framework (SCIPAB, SCQA, STAR) to actually communicate them.
**Example:** Before drafting an incident summary: Who was affected, what happened, when, where in the system, why it occurred, how it was resolved — answered first, then structured into an answer-first SCQA summary for leadership.
**Source:** [What is the 5 Ws (and 5W1H) framework? — LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/what-is-the-5-ws-5w1h-framework/)

### Rhetorical Appeals
**Full name:** Ethos, Logos, Pathos, Kairos — Aristotle's appeals, used as a board-level check
**Source:** Aristotle, *Rhetoric* (c. 350 BC), which sets out ethos, pathos, and logos as the three "artistic proofs." *Kairos* — the right thing at the right moment — comes from the same rhetorical tradition but is not one of Aristotle's three; it is usually treated as a separate concept and is included here because timing is the appeal business cases most often miss. Part 4 covers the same material as a weighting layer applied across the other frameworks; this entry is the version you fill in on a single board.
**Components:**
- Ethos — why this audience should trust the source: credibility, track record, standing to make the ask.
- Logos — the logical case: data, evidence, the reasoning chain.
- Pathos — what is at stake for the audience personally: consequence, cost, the human win.
- Kairos — why now: the timing that makes this the right moment to raise it.
**When to use:** Pressure-testing a persuasive case that is already drafted — the check that finds an argument leaning entirely on data when the room also needs to trust you, or entirely on urgency when the room wants proof.
**When not to use:** As the argument itself. This checks a case; it does not build or sequence one. An empty appeal is a gap to close inside another framework, not a board to present.
**Example:** A budget ask reviewed against the four: Logos is strong (three quarters of cost data), Ethos is implicit (the team delivered the last two programs — say so), Pathos is absent (nobody has named what happens to the roadmap if this is declined), Kairos is absent (nothing explains why this quarter rather than next). The two gaps are what to write.
**Source:** [Aristotle's Rhetoric — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-rhetoric/)

### Five Factors
**Full name:** The Five Factors — The Way, Heaven, Earth, Command, Method
**Source:** Sun Tzu, *The Art of War* (c. 5th century BC), Chapter 1. Sun Tzu names five factors to be weighed before committing to a contest — 道 *dào* (the Way / moral influence), 天 *tiān* (Heaven / climate and timing), 地 *dì* (Earth / terrain), 將 *jiàng* (Command / the general), 法 *fǎ* (Method / discipline and organization). Note: the text is a genuine and much-translated classic, but its use as a *communication* framework is this catalog's adaptation, not an established business-communication model.
**Components:**
1. The Way — whether the people who must execute believe in it; alignment between leadership's intent and the team's will. Sun Tzu ranks this first: a plan without buy-in fails regardless of the rest.
2. Heaven — the conditions you do not control: market cycle, seasonality, regulatory calendar, competitor moves already in motion.
3. Earth — the terrain you actually occupy: distribution, cost structure, existing relationships, distance to the customer.
4. Command — who is leading, and whether they have the judgment, discipline, and credibility to see it through.
5. Method — the organization behind the effort: structure, supply chain, budget discipline. Whether the machine can deliver what the plan promises.
**When to use:** Deciding whether to enter or continue a competitive fight — a market entry, a bid, a contested initiative — before resources are committed.
**When not to use:** Routine updates or single asks. This is an assessment you perform and then present; it is not itself a way to sequence a message.
**Example:** A market-entry review: the Way is weak (the field team does not believe the segment is winnable), Heaven is favorable (a competitor just exited), Earth is mixed (no local distribution), Command is strong, Method is unproven. The weak Way is the finding — and the reason to delay rather than fund.
**Source:** [The Art of War — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_War)

### Book of Five Rings
**Full name:** Go Rin no Sho — Ground, Water, Fire, Wind, Void
**Source:** Miyamoto Musashi, *The Book of Five Rings* (c. 1645), written as five scrolls — Earth/Ground, Water, Fire, Wind, and Void — each treating strategy from a different angle rather than as sequential steps. Note: as with the Art of War entry, the text is genuine; framing its five scrolls as a presentation arc is this catalog's adaptation.
**Components:**
1. Ground — the foundation: core principles, non-negotiables, the basic capability you build from. Deliberately unglamorous; everything else rests on it.
2. Water — adaptability: how the plan takes the shape of its container, and the specific ways you will adjust as conditions change.
3. Fire — the direct contest: competitive tactics, timing an advantage, where you engage head-on. The most decisive part of the arc.
4. Wind — study of rivals: what other approaches are doing, and what you are deliberately doing differently or borrowing.
5. Void — the judgment that goes beyond technique: where you rely on experience because the situation does not fit a formula. Used sparingly, to justify a non-obvious call.
**When to use:** Long-form strategy narratives that need to show foundation and adaptability and competitive awareness, and then land on a judgment call.
**When not to use:** Short asks and status updates — the arc needs five beats of room, and compressing it strips out the thing that makes it worth using.
**Example:** An annual strategy deck: Ground states the two capabilities the business will not trade away; Water covers the three conditions that would change the plan; Fire names the one segment to contest directly; Wind compares two rivals' approaches; Void argues for a bet the data cannot yet justify.
**Source:** [The Book of Five Rings — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_Five_Rings)

### Six Thinking Hats
**Full name:** Six Thinking Hats — White, Red, Black, Yellow, Green, Blue
**Source:** Edward de Bono, *Six Thinking Hats* (1985). The method is "parallel thinking": rather than participants arguing from fixed positions, the whole group deliberately occupies one mode at a time, so caution, optimism, and invention each get a fair hearing instead of competing.
**Components:**
1. White Hat — facts and data only. What is known, what is missing, what would we need to find out. No opinions.
2. Red Hat — gut reaction and emotion, stated plainly and without justification.
3. Black Hat — risks, flaws, and reasons this could fail. The deliberate skeptic, so caution is a role rather than one dissenting voice.
4. Yellow Hat — benefits and best case, argued with the same rigor the Black Hat brought to risk.
5. Green Hat — alternatives and new ideas. What has not been proposed yet.
6. Blue Hat — process control: what the review concluded and what happens next. Usually the framing board, the closing board, or both.
**When to use:** Structured group reviews of a decision or idea, especially where discussion habitually collapses into all-criticism or all-enthusiasm before the option is understood.
**When not to use:** Solo drafting — the value is in a group changing mode together. A single author can use it as a checklist, but the mechanism is social.
**Example:** A go/no-go review run in sequence: Blue frames the decision, White lays out what the pilot actually measured, Red captures that the team is uneasy, Black names the two failure modes, Yellow argues the upside case, Green proposes a smaller pilot instead, Blue closes with the decision and owner.
**Source:** [Six Thinking Hats — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Thinking_Hats)

### Tao Te Ching
**Full name:** The Way, Wu Wei, and the case for restraint
**Source:** The *Tao Te Ching*, attributed to Laozi (c. 4th century BC), whose central practical idea — 無為 *wú wéi*, usually rendered "non-action" or "effortless action" — is not passivity but declining to force what will resolve on its own, and acting with a situation's grain rather than against it. Note: the text is genuine and foundational to Taoism; using it as a business-communication framework is this catalog's adaptation, and it is the furthest of these from an established model.
**Components:**
- The Way — the situation's natural tendency if left alone. State what happens with no intervention.
- Wu Wei — the smallest action, or non-action, that aligns with that tendency rather than fighting it.
- Why less wins here — the case for restraint over more force, more process, or more control.
**When to use:** Arguing to simplify, remove a step, delay, or deliberately not intervene, when the instinct in the room is to add effort. It gives restraint a structure, which is what restraint usually lacks in a meeting.
**When not to use:** When something does need to be done. This board argues against action; pair it with a SCIPAB or SCQA board if you also have to say what should happen instead.
**Example:** A proposal to add an approval gate after two bad releases: the Way is that both failures came from a rushed cutover the team has already stopped doing; Wu Wei is to leave the process alone and let the change that already worked settle; less wins because a gate would slow all twenty releases to catch a pattern that has already ended.
**Source:** [Tao Te Ching — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao_Te_Ching)

## Part 4 — The Rhetorical Layer: Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Most of the frameworks above are structural — they tell you how to *sequence* a message. Ethos, Pathos, and Logos are not a competing structure; they tell you what to *weight* inside whichever sequence you choose. Every framework above has a slot for a recommendation or a "so what" — this layer is about what that slot is made of.

There is also a **Rhetorical Appeals** entry in Part 3, covering the same appeals plus *kairos* as a four-part check you fill in on a single board. This part is the deeper treatment and the cross-framework weighting guidance; that entry is the working version.

**Origin:** Set out by Aristotle in *Rhetoric* (c. 350 BC) as the three "artistic proofs" — the persuasive resources a speaker builds themselves, as opposed to hard evidence. A fourth, less-cited term from the same tradition, *kairos*, refers to timing — saying the right thing at the right moment — and is worth knowing even though it doesn't get its own pillar in most modern treatments.

**Components:**
- **Ethos** — an appeal to credibility: your track record, expertise, or the rigor of your process. This is what makes an audience trust the source, independent of the argument itself.
- **Pathos** — an appeal to emotion: urgency, ambition, concern, aspiration. This is what makes an audience *care* enough to act.
- **Logos** — an appeal to logic and evidence: data, cause-and-effect reasoning, proof. This is what makes an audience believe the argument is *sound*.

Aristotle's point, echoed across the rhetoric literature, is that persuasion rarely runs on one appeal alone — the strongest communication blends all three, in a proportion that fits the audience and the stakes.

**Where each appeal lives inside the frameworks you already have:**

| Framework | Where Logos lives | Where Ethos lives | Where Pathos lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| SCIPAB | Implication, Position (data-backed) | Position (your judgment call) | Benefit (the payoff, made vivid) |
| Pyramid/SCQA | The Answer and its MECE support | Implicit in the rigor of the structure itself | Rarely used deliberately — by design |
| STAR | Result (quantified outcome) | Action (what you personally did) | Situation (the stakes at the start) |
| SWOT | The whole grid is a logos exercise | — | — (pair with another framework to add this) |
| OGSM | Measures | Strategies (credibility of the chosen path) | Objective (the ambition, stated to inspire) |
| Five Factors | Heaven, Earth, Method (conditions and capability) | Command (who is leading, and their record) | The Way (whether the team believes in it) |
| Book of Five Rings | Ground, Wind (foundation and rival comparison) | Void (a judgment call you are staking your name on) | Fire (the decisive engagement) |
| Six Thinking Hats | White Hat | Blue Hat (control of the process itself) | Red Hat (feeling, given an explicit slot) |
| Tao Te Ching | Why less wins (the cost of intervening) | Wu Wei (restraint reads as confidence) | The Way (what is lost by forcing it) |

**Guidance for VP-level communication specifically:** senior audiences default to trusting logos first, granting ethos once your track record is established, and treating heavy pathos with suspicion — it reads as trying too hard, or as compensating for a weak case. A workable default weighting for exec audiences is roughly logos-heavy, ethos-supporting, pathos-minimal and deliberate — used only at specific moments (an OGSM Objective, a SCIPAB Benefit) rather than spread throughout.

**When to lean into each:**
- **Logos** — budget asks, technical decisions, anything where the audience will independently check your numbers.
- **Ethos** — the first time you're presenting to a given audience, or on politically sensitive topics where trust matters as much as the data.
- **Pathos** — change management, vision-setting, or any ask for discretionary effort that data alone won't justify.

**Common failure modes:**
- All logos, no ethos or pathos — technically correct, but reads as a report rather than a recommendation; doesn't move anyone to act.
- Ethos with no logos — "trust me" without the backing data; a VP-level audience will ask for the numbers regardless.
- Too much pathos — undermines credibility with analytical, senior audiences; reads as manipulative rather than persuasive.

**Sources:** [Aristotle's Rhetoric — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-rhetoric/) · [Modes of persuasion — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modes_of_persuasion)

## Part 5 — Communication Intent Map (Clarify / Decide / Act / Engage)

This framing was shared by a colleague as part of an internal agent design (the "LOGOS Strategic Comm Agent") rather than drawn from a published source — it's included here because it's a fast, practical way to pick a framework: start from what you actually need the audience to do, then choose from the frameworks built for that.

- **Clarify** — you need to understand or explain a situation before anything else. → **5 Ws/5W1H** (gather the full picture first), **SWOT** (assess internal/external factors), **Five Factors** (assess a competitive contest), **Six Thinking Hats** (when the clarifying has to happen as a group).
- **Decide** — you need a specific decision, choice, or approval made. → **DECIDE**, **Pyramid Principle/SCQA** (when the audience just wants the answer), **OGSM** (when the "decision" is really about setting direction), **Tao Te Ching** (when the right decision is to not act).
- **Act** — you need the audience to do something, without necessarily building a full case first. → **PSAB**, **SCIPAB** when the case still has to be made.
- **Engage** — you need buy-in or emotional investment, not just comprehension. → **ABT**, **STAR** (as a proof-of-impact story), **Book of Five Rings** (for a strategy narrative with a judgment call at the top), with **WIIFM** and **Rhetorical Appeals** applied as lenses across any of the above.

**SCIPAB** sits across Decide and Act — it's built to move an audience from hesitation to a specific action, which is why it's the most load-bearing framework in the original set of seven.

## Notes on Use

Several of these frameworks solve different problems and are meant to be combined, not chosen exclusively:

- **SWOT** and **Five Factors** both produce analysis; **SCQA** or **SCIPAB** turns that analysis into a message. They aren't competitors. SWOT assesses a position, Five Factors assesses a contest.
- **STAR** looks backward (what happened); **OGSM** looks forward (what we're aiming for). A strong quarterly business review often uses both: OGSM to restate the plan, STAR to report against it.
- **DECIDE** and **PSAB** are more specialized cousins of **SCIPAB** — DECIDE when the ask is explicitly a decision among named options, PSAB when the case can be made in four beats instead of six.
- **Six Thinking Hats** is the only entry built for a room rather than a document. Use it to *produce* the material, then present the conclusions through a structural framework.
- **Rhetorical Appeals** is a check, not a structure — run it over a finished draft. **Tao Te Ching** is its mirror image on content: it argues for less, so it needs a partner framework whenever something does have to be done.
- **OGSM** and **Book of Five Rings** both set direction. OGSM is the quantified version — objectives and measures. Five Rings is the version for when the strategy rests on adaptability and a judgment call the numbers don't yet support.
- **Ethos/Pathos/Logos** and **WIIFM** both sit underneath the sequential frameworks rather than replacing them — one is about what to weight (logic vs. credibility vs. emotion), the other is about who you're weighting it for. Neither is a message structure on its own.
- Part 5's intent map (Clarify/Decide/Act/Engage) is the fastest entry point if you're starting from scratch: figure out the intent first, then pick the framework, then decide how much of Ethos/Pathos/Logos and WIIFM to layer in.
